I am a PhD candidate in Economics at Jönköping International Business School. I am on the 2026–27 academic job market.

I am an applied microeconomist studying how political institutions, representation and group identity shape public policy and development outcomes for historically marginalised communities, with applications in India and cross-country settings. My job market paper uses India's 2008 Delimitation, the world's largest redistricting exercise, as a natural experiment and satellite data to show that electing indigenous representatives to state legislatures increases local forest cover, with the largest gains near mining sites and in sparsely forested areas. I work at the intersection of political economy and development economics, combining causal inference methods, including difference-in-differences, event studies, synthetic control, instrumental variables and matching, with large administrative, survey, satellite and text data.

Job Market Paper

Who Governs the Forest? Politician Identity and Forest Conservation

This paper asks whether the identity of elected representatives affects how forests are governed. I exploit India's 2008 Delimitation, the world's largest redistricting exercise, as a natural experiment that changed the reservation status of state assembly constituencies from open competition to seats reserved exclusively for historically marginalized indigenous communities, thereby generating plausibly exogenous variation in politician identity. Using a staggered difference-in-differences design and satellite-derived forest cover data from 2001 to 2020, I find that indigenous political representation increases local forest cover by 10.5%. The effect is concentrated in villages with low baseline forest cover and is largest near mining sites, declining monotonically with distance. I also show that political quotas disrupt the political equilibrium: indigenous representation brings younger, more female winners into office through more competitive elections, reshaping who holds power, not just who holds office. I further find no evidence that these gains erode once electoral quotas are withdrawn. These findings speak to a fundamental question in political economy: who governs shapes what is governed, and democratic representation of historically excluded indigenous communities can deliver meaningful environmental dividends, with direct implications for climate policy and debates on indigenous governance across the developing world.
